# Losing the tail — the man behind the captain

*#1062 slice 2, the mirror of [the observation walk](../features/haven-interior-walk.md)'s tail. Shipped
2026-09-17.*

> Owner, 2026-09-01: *"following one of the customers covertly without them noticing us would be classic spy
> / detective stuff :-D … or trying to lose a tail our selves :-D"*

Slice 1 gave the captain somebody to follow. This is the other half: **somebody mundane and human is behind
you**, ashore at a haven, and neither the HUD nor the book will say a word about it until you work it out
yourself.

---

## 1. Who puts him there

Nobody, almost always. Two things open the door, and both are state the game already keeps:

- **An outfit's #715 folder on you, at the band where it wants a face.** Illegal heat is owed to an
  *operator*, and `IllegalHeat.TheGateWantsAFace` is the rung where a company stops treating a hull as
  paperwork. A man walking twenty paces behind you is that sentence with legs on. Two shipped acts already
  bank heat at a berth: the walk-in's femme-fatale setup (*"a customs post that has been waiting for the ship
  this errand was flown in"*) and selling the compromising chip at a dark-web desk.
- **Slice 1's own failure.** Tail one of the bar's named regulars and get *clocked* doing it, and you have
  advertised what you do for a living. The evening answers.

The folder **cools by itself** — an hour of sim time a point, and only while you are off that outfit's
ground. So the counter-play to being followed at a berth is the owner's own ruling about this meter said back
to him: *get out*.

> **Not #804.** The issue offers the guard-suspicion ladder as a candidate, and ashore there is no such thing:
> it is an escort count on a patrolled floor of an underground complex, spawned and stepped only from a
> surface excursion. A berth has neither.

## 2. What he does

He comes in **after you** — dealt on the *concourse* side of the room's own doorway and walking in behind
you, never on the spot you are standing on. What he does once he is in has **two shapes**, and the room
chooses between them:

**In a room off the ring — the bar, or the observation walk — he takes a POST.** A standing place inside
that room, against the room's own stone, with a line of sight to you, **nearest the doorway he came in by**.
Never the counter, and never a top: he has not ordered, and a seat would make him a patron. He walks there at
his own 2 du/s and **stays** — a post is kept until the *line* breaks, so he does not shuffle along the wall
after a captain crossing the floor. The chair reading is then exactly what it was written to be: you sit
facing the door, and he is the man by the door who has not ordered.

**Out on the ring he keeps a distance band** — no nearer than `PatrolBeat.NoticeDu` (this game's own range
for *one person registering that another is standing there*) and no further than `FootTail.LegibleDu` (the
range a body on a deck is legible at all).

> **Why two, and how the room chooses.** Measured, at every haven: the bar reaches about **twenty-three deck
> units** in its longest walkable line from its own doorway, and the band's far edge is **thirty**. A man
> told to keep a 9–30 du band in a room that size cannot hold it as his subject crosses the floor — which is
> what #1229/#1231 watched happen: the stone pushed him out through the one doorway, he lost a captain he had
> never been in the room with, and he gave up silently three seconds later. The ring reaches well past thirty
> from the same doorway, so out there the band is keepable and is kept.

**When you leave the room, he waits one look and follows you through the same doorway.** That is what makes
the two-door tell a *sequence* — posted inside, you go, he comes out after you, you take a second doorway, he
comes through that one — rather than an accident of where he happened to be standing beforehand.

He never confronts you, never speaks, never blocks a door, and is never put on a pathfinder an Old One could
be put on. He is drawn as a figure with **no name over him** — #832's own "draw less when you cannot resolve
it" — because the canon he is written out of ends *never a face*.

**And a man who cannot see you does not know where to go.** While he has you, he holds his place. Blind, the
only place he has any reason to walk to is where he last had you — and, if he was posted, a *wall spot* with
a line to that place rather than the open floor of it.

## 3. Finding him — two pieces of craft

| The move | What it costs | The line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sit in a seat that can see the door.** Walking is refused while seated, so this costs you the floor for as long as it takes — twelve of the game's own looks, nine seconds at warp 1. | a sit | *"From this chair you can see the door. So can the man who came in after you, and he has not ordered."* |
| **Double back through a room with two ways out.** The count is the FIRE CODE's own (`UndergroundComplex.FireCodeMinExits`) and it counts DISTINCT doorways, so a man loitering in one for a whole watch never adds up. A locked leaf is not a doorway. | a detour | *"The same grey coat, two doors running. Nobody's errand takes them through both."* |

**Until one of those lands, nothing in this game says one word.** No pulse, no card, no badge, no book entry.
The one thing that gives him away is the figure on the floor, which is where a gumshoe's evidence is supposed
to be.

## 4. Losing him

**Break his line for as long as it took to find him** — twelve looks again, one number read from both ends.
What breaks a line ashore is *stone*: the bar's south wall with its one doorway, the ring's nine sealed
edges, the gangway, and at Selene Gate a glass tube with a blind end.

> **The audit's correction.** #1062 sketches "a door closed between you" and "a lift timed at the close".
> Ashore this game has neither: a haven's collision field is built from **walls only**, no station door
> carries an interlock, an auto-door never blocks a passage, and there is no lift at a berth at all. What a
> berth does have is a **gangway he will not walk down** — he is paid to put a face to a hull, not to board
> one — so walking aboard your own ship is the timed close, and it is made of geography.

Lost, he goes, and if you had noticed him you are told once:

> *"The corridor behind you is only a corridor. Whoever it was is asking the wrong floor about you."*

…and the field book keeps it, filed under the **place** and never a face:

> 👁 *a tail, lost at SELENE GATE — a grey coat, never a face*

**A captain who never noticed him is told nothing at all, ever.** He stood about, he gave up, he left, and no
pulse, card or book entry in this game will ever mention that anybody was there.

## 4b. Failing forward — the burn

> #1062: *"Fail Forward, never game-over — the place you were going is burned, and leading a tail to the
> dead-drop is how a dead-drop dies."*

The dead drop is on a moon and this figure is not, so the ashore equivalent is what a captain actually goes
to a berth to do quietly: **take a favour across a contact's table**, or **buy a code off a fence at a
dark-web desk**. Both already run through one strike-off (`BlackOpsKey.ThePortHasDealtOne`), and the burn
hangs on that one seam so the two of them cannot come to two views of what being watched costs.

**Do either with a man on the floor behind you and the place is burned.** Deterministic — no roll, the
condition is simply that somebody was there to see it. **And nothing whatever is said at the moment.** You
get what you came for and walk out.

You find out the next time you walk in, and the place has nothing for you:

> *"Tidy, in the way a place is after somebody has been through it first."*

> 👁 *SELENE GATE — walked before you got there, by somebody who knew where to walk*

Filed under the same **place** as the losing note, so THREADS stacks the evening in the order it happened —
*he was behind me, and then this place had been gone through*. The book never draws that line; it says what
happened, and the captain draws it.

The burn costs **one visit's worth of what that port deals**, and it is spent by being told: there is no
window to miss. Shake him first and it costs nothing at all, which is the counter-play and the reason the
nine seconds of stone are worth spending.

## 5. The laws it is built on

- **One sightline oracle.** `SurfaceCollision.HasLineOfSight` — reached through `PatrolBeat.EyesOn` and
  `FootTail.InPlainSight`, the same one slice 1 uses. There is exactly one declared in all of `src/`, and a
  guard sweeps for it.
- **No dice.** Not one, anywhere in the half. Every question is arithmetic on a range, a clock and a count of
  doorways. (Slice 1 *does* roll — the captain tailing somebody asks #436's eye, because the question there is
  whether a man happened to glance over his shoulder. Nobody is glancing here.)
- **#793's seam, filled.** `FootTail` shipped as a seam with its own note: *"Nothing in the game tails the
  captain yet … the day a watcher is built it has one place to declare itself."* This is the first thing in
  the game that declares itself there, and the held bar that pen has drawn for nobody since #793 finally has
  somebody under it.
- **Fail Forward.** There is no game over in it and no combat in it. The worst thing that happens is that you
  never notice.
- **Two behaviours, and the room chooses by measurement.** A post in a room off the ring, a band on the ring —
  never by the room's name. `TheTailBehindYou.PostsAlong` cuts a wall into body-wide slices and offers the
  middle of each one body clear of the stone, on the captain's side; the client keeps the ones the whole room
  allows and takes the one nearest the doorway he came in by. No dice, no search, no second pathfinder, and
  the walls are the deck plan's own — so a room redrawn tomorrow gives the same man a different corner with
  no edit anywhere.
